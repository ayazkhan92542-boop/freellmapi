# "The Pharaoh Who Edited His Own War" — script v1 (2026-11-23)
Topic: Battle of Kadesh (c. 1274 BC), Ramesses II vs the Hittites under Muwatalli II, and the peace treaty that followed.
Target ~62-66 s at ~3.7 words/s (~240 words). Daytime sets: Syrian plain + Orontes river, Kadesh walls, Egyptian camp,
temple wall with carved reliefs, UN hall.

## Facts + sources
- c. 1274 BC, near Kadesh on the Orontes (modern Syria). Ramesses II marched north with four divisions (Amun, Ra, Ptah, Seth).
- In the Egyptian account two Shasu nomads told him the Hittite army was far away near Aleppo; they were Hittite plants.
  Ramesses pushed ahead with the Amun division and camped near Kadesh.
- Hittite chariots attacked from behind the city, routed the Ra division on the march, then hit the Egyptian camp.
  Often called probably the largest chariot battle ever fought (thousands of chariots).
- Ramesses' own account (Poem of Pentaur / Bulletin) says he fought almost alone with the help of the god Amun;
  reinforcements (the Ne'arin) arrived in time. The result was inconclusive: Egypt withdrew, the Hittites kept Kadesh;
  both sides claimed victory.
- Ramesses had the battle carved and written as a triumph on several temples (Abu Simbel, Karnak, Luxor, the Ramesseum, Abydos).
- About 15-16 years later (c. 1259 BC; sources vary 1269-1258 BC) Egypt and the Hittites (Hattusili III) signed the
  oldest known surviving peace treaty. Turkey gave the UN a copper replica in 1970; it is displayed at UN Headquarters
  by the Security Council chamber. The original clay tablet is in the Istanbul Archaeological Museums.
- Sources: Wikipedia "Battle of Kadesh"; Britannica "Battle of Kadesh"; World History Encyclopedia "The Battle of Kadesh &
  the First Peace Treaty"; Fordham Ancient History Sourcebook (Kadesh texts); UN Gifts "Replica of Peace Treaty between
  Hattusilis and Ramses II" (un.org/ungifts/node/615); Turkish MFA note on the UN replica.

## Script
Ramesses the Second nearly lost probably the biggest chariot battle in history, so he carved it on his temples as a huge victory.
It's twelve seventy-four B C, near the city of Kadesh, in what's now Syria, and the young pharaoh is marching north with four armies.
Two desert nomads walk into his camp and tell him the enemy Hittites are far away, and terrified of him.
Ramesses is delighted, so he races ahead with just one army and sets up camp.
The nomads were spies.
Thousands of Hittite chariots burst out from behind the city and smash straight through his second army.
Then they crash into his camp.
According to Ramesses, his soldiers ran away, so he jumped on his chariot and fought the whole enemy alone.
Luckily, a fresh Egyptian army arrives just in time.
Unluckily, by the end, nobody has really won, and the Hittites still have Kadesh.
You see, an ancient king couldn't post an update, so the walls of his temples were his news feed.
It's like a highlight reel: nobody posts the part where they fell over.
So Ramesses carved the battle on temple after temple, starring himself, giant-sized, crushing the enemy.
Sixteen years later, Egypt and the Hittites signed the oldest peace treaty we've ever found.
And today a copy of it hangs at the United Nations, right outside the Security Council.
