# "The One-Eyed, One-Legged War Hero" — script v1 (2026-11-22)
Topic: Cher Ami, the US Army Signal Corps homing pigeon credited with the "Lost Battalion" message, October 1918.
Target ~60-65 s at ~3.7 words/s (~230 words). Daytime sets only (autumn forest, HQ loft, medal ceremony, museum).

## Facts + sources
- Oct 1918, Argonne Forest (France): ~550 men of the US 77th Division under Maj. Charles Whittlesey were cut off and
  surrounded ("the Lost Battalion"), then hit by an American artillery barrage by mistake.
- They had no radio; messages went by carrier pigeon. Earlier pigeons were shot down.
- The message credited to Cher Ami: "We are along the road parallel 276.4. Our own artillery is dropping a barrage
  directly on us. For heaven's sake stop it."
- Army accounts: Cher Ami was shot through the breast, lost an eye, and the leg carrying the message was left hanging
  by a tendon; flew ~25 miles in ~25 minutes. Soldiers carved a small wooden leg. France awarded the Croix de Guerre
  with palm. The bird is preserved at the Smithsonian (National Museum of American History).
- Called "she" for about a century; in 2021 Smithsonian DNA testing confirmed Cher Ami was male. "Cher ami" is the
  masculine French for "dear friend" (feminine would be "chère amie").
- DISPUTED: the National Archives notes nothing conclusive ties this pigeon to the Lost Battalion message, and
  Smithsonian magazine says the shelling had already stopped minutes before the bird reached its loft. So the script
  never says the pigeon stopped the barrage or "saved" the men; it says he delivered the message and the men survived.
- Sources: Wikipedia "Cher Ami"; Britannica "Cher Ami"; Smithsonian (si.edu object nmah_425415, Smithsonian magazine
  2021/2024); National Archives Prologue blog "Unsung Heroes of World War I: The Carrier Pigeons" (2023);
  DocsTeach (National Archives) pigeon message from Whittlesey; History.com (C. Klein).

## Script
A pigeon with one eye and one leg once won a medal for bravery in a war.
It's October nineteen eighteen, in a French forest, and about five hundred American soldiers are completely surrounded.
Then it gets worse: their own army starts shelling them by mistake.
They don't have a radio. Their only way to call for help is a basket of pigeons.
The first pigeon gets shot down. So does the second.
The last one is called Cher Ami.
He takes off, and every rifle in the forest opens fire at anything with wings.
He's hit in the chest, loses an eye, and his leg is left hanging, but he keeps flying.
Twenty-five minutes later, he lands at headquarters with the message still attached: our own artillery is dropping a barrage directly on us, for heaven's sake, stop it.
You see, a homing pigeon doesn't need a map.
It's raised in one loft, and wherever you let it go, it uses the sun and the Earth's magnetic field to fly straight home, like a phone with only one address saved.
The soldiers survived, and they carved him a tiny wooden leg.
Luckily, France gave him a medal.
Unluckily, for a hundred years, everyone called him a she.
In twenty twenty-one, a DNA test finally checked. Cher Ami was a boy, which his name, French for dear friend, had been saying all along.
